Which Side Did It Hit?

The ball sometimes clips the side of a brick and carries on straight through the wall. Today you work out which way it should have gone.

What you're making

A ball that bounces off the side of a brick as convincingly as it bounces off the bottom.

One short function, twelve lines long. The same twelve lines decide whether a player lands on a platform or slides down its side.

New this session

Flipping vy is a guess, and it is wrong about a third of the time. It assumes the ball arrived from above or below. Come in from the side and the ball needs vx flipped instead.

So work out which edge it came in through. In one frame the ball only moves a few pixels — so whichever of the brick's edges it has barely got past is the one it just crossed. The edge it is deep inside, it must have been lined up with all along.

Four subtractions and a comparison. Measure how far past the left, the right, the top and the bottom it has got, take the smallest across and the smallest down, and flip whichever is shallower.

This is exactly how landing on a platform works, which is why it is worth getting right now rather than in the middle of the Geometry Dash arc.

Stuck?

Hint 1 — why is the shallowest edge the right one?

Because of how little happens in a sixtieth of a second.

A ball at 340 pixels a second moves under six pixels per frame. So when it is found overlapping a brick, it can only have got a few pixels past whichever edge it came through — there has not been time for more.

The other edges it is 'past' by a lot, because it was already lined up with them before it ever arrived. Deep means it was always there; shallow means it just happened.

Hint 2 — what do the four sums mean?

Each one is a depth. Take from_left:

(ball["x"] + ball["w"]) is the ball's right-hand side. brick["x"] is the brick's left-hand side. Subtract and you get how far the ball's right side has pushed past the brick's left edge.

If the ball is only just poking in from the left, that is a small number. If it is right through the middle of the brick, it is a big one.

Hint 3 — what is None?

Python's word for nothing at all, and the usual way to say "no answer yet".

first_hit = None means no brick has been hit this frame. If one is, it goes in that variable. Afterwards, if first_hit is not None: asks whether anything ever turned up.

You met it in Session 5 without knowing: a function with no return hands back None.

def bounce_off_brick(ball, brick):
    from_left = (ball["x"] + ball["w"]) - brick["x"]
    from_right = (brick["x"] + brick["w"]) - ball["x"]
    from_top = (ball["y"] + ball["h"]) - brick["y"]
    from_bottom = (brick["y"] + brick["h"]) - ball["y"]

    across = min(from_left, from_right)
    down = min(from_top, from_bottom)

    if across < down:
        ball["vx"] = -ball["vx"]
    else:
        ball["vy"] = -ball["vy"]

Challenge

Use it on the walls too. The screen edges are just four very large bricks. Write them as brick-shaped dicts — one down each side, one across the top — and let the same function handle them.

Everything gets simpler when the walls stop being a special case. And it sets up the Geometry Dash arc, where the ground is just another block.

Harder: a brick that takes two hits. It only vanishes on the second, but the ball must bounce properly both times. Nothing about the bounce changes — only what happens to the brick afterwards.
